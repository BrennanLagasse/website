Why the background never converges

Several hundred optimizers descend a loss surface that keeps moving underneath them.

10 Nov 2026 4 min read

Each point behind this text is a two-parameter model trained on noisy gradients by one of three optimizers: momentum gradient descent, AdamW or Muon. The surface is a shallow bowl with seven wells and two ridges cut into it. Every well drifts, deepens and fades on its own slow clock.

Because the objective is non-stationary, convergence is only ever temporary. Points settle into a basin, then the basin shallows or merges with a neighbor, and they spill over the saddle toward whatever is deepest now.

Three ways down a hill

The optimizers handle the moving target differently. Momentum accelerates down steep walls and overshoots; when a basin moves, it carries its velocity past the new minimum before turning back. AdamW takes steps of roughly fixed size per coordinate, so it moves at the same pace on gentle and steep slopes, and its weight decay pulls each point toward the origin. Muon's steps have a fixed length, so it crosses flat plateaus as quickly as steep slopes.

With only two parameters, Muon's orthogonalization reduces to normalizing the update: orth(x) = x / ‖x‖. That makes it the easiest of the three to reason about here. It also means Muon points never quite stop: they jitter back and forth across the bottom of a basin, in steps whose length is set by the learning rate.

What to look for

Watch the grey points. They have stopped moving for the moment, so they are the closest thing this system has to a converged model. When a basin starts to fade, the momentum points carry the most speed out of it. AdamW points settle slightly off the true bottom of each well, displaced toward the center of the screen. That offset is the weight decay: it adds a constant pull toward the origin, so the resting point balances the gradient against it.
