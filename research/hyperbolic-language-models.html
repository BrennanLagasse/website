Language is a tree, so embed it in a hyperbolic space

Small language models with hyperbolic prediction heads, and why ordinary Euclidean embedding models seem to approximate them anyway.

22 Oct 2026 Graph and Geometric Learning Lab, Yale 6 min read

Much of language is organized hierarchically. Words sit inside phrases, phrases inside clauses, and concepts inside broader concepts: a poodle is a dog, which is a mammal, which is an animal. Trees like these grow exponentially: the number of nodes at depth d grows like bd. Euclidean space only grows polynomially, so embedding a large tree in it without distortion takes many dimensions.

Hyperbolic space has the opposite problem, which suits trees. The circumference of a hyperbolic circle grows exponentially with its radius, so there is room for every branch. A tree can be embedded in the hyperbolic plane with arbitrarily low distortion, in just two dimensions.

What we tried

We pretrained small language models whose final embedding head lives in hyperbolic space instead of Euclidean space, then analyzed the geometry the models learned. Separately, we built and benchmarked a retrieval-augmented generation pipeline on top of a fully hyperbolic text embedding model, to see whether the geometry helps when retrieval follows a hierarchy.

The surprising part

On the theory side, we worked out what properties a hyperbolic embedding space should have if it really reflects the tree structure of language. Then we checked common Euclidean embedding models for those same properties, and found that in practice they approximate them. One reading is that these models are already trying to be hyperbolic, and spend dimensions to make up for the space they lack.
